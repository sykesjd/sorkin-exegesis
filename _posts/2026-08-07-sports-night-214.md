I think it's fair to say, given what we've seen up to this point, that season two of _Sports Night_ is a season of plot thread indecision. The Casey-Dana storyline, set up to be the main focus of the season at the start, unceremoniously wrapped up halfway through the season, and the exploration of Dan's entry into therapy appears to have been dropped like a hot rock at some point. This episode manages to make another charged plot thread prematurely go the way of the dodo, presumably in the service of elevating yet another plot thread which also quixotically promises to last the rest of the season. I honestly don't know what to trust anymore with this show, so I'm left to continue taking it an episode at a time like I pretty much already have been. On that note, let's get into it.

For as much airtime as I give about the Sorkin television throughline of work family's being true family, Sam Donovan represents an interesting subversion of that rule. Where others in the Sorkin universe see continually doing their job as a necessary component of showing their love and respect for their colleagues, Sam simply takes the role of helicoptering in to do his job then pulling out as soon as his job is done. "I'll help you, but don't expect me to become one of you," he essentially said to our merry band of heroes. The closest analogous character I can think of in _The West Wing_ would be Bruno Gianelli of the Bartlet re-elect, who makes a point of saying his contract has ended after the election is called. The difference here, of course, is that, as he says here, Sam explicitly makes the effort to be such a helicopter because he would be like one of them otherwise, which is a possibility that scares him. Perhaps he'll be made to confront his fear?

And with that, another _Sports Night_ plot thread is inexplicably cut short — that is the last we see of Sam Donovan and what would otherwise have been a promising exploration of his continued relationship with the Sports Night staff. Left in its wake is presumably what's made to be the primary plot thread for the rest of the season, which is the overly-charged game of Conflict Ball between Natalie and Jeremy. This episode represents another entry in a line of episodes wherein either Peter Krause or Josh Charles — in this case, the former — takes on the role of comic foil to an otherwise serious episode, and once again I'm unfortunately disinclined to consider the delighful performance provided sufficient to save the episode. The heavy-handed treatment of Natalie's mental degradation in the wake of her breakup with Jeremy, while showing notes of logical consistency, overall leaves a sour taste with its rather derivative formula.
