And those where the final words Aaron Sorkin would write for _Sports Night_.

ABC cancelled the show after two seasons due to, fittingly enough, ratings not being up to their standards. That didn't stop the production companies for _Sports Night_, however, from shopping the show around to have it continue. HBO, Showtime, and the USA Network were all reportedly in the running to continue the show, with Showtime being the furthest advanced in negotiations. The talks with Showtime even got as far as an offer for two seasons of shows at $850 thousand per episode.

However, Mr. Sorkin then told Showtime that he wouldn't be joining the show in its move.

It was an understandable call from the man, considering he was already running two shows both at the same time for the past year — not to mention he was expecting his first child that coming November. To be expected to continue running two shows simultaneously simply wasn't feasible, and Mr. Sorkin knew it.

He would have been leaving the show in capable hands, if I may say so myself. I was quick to dunk on them in most of my entries for the episodes where Mr. Sorkin eschewed writing credit, but all in all they availed themselves well in the realm of writing for a half-hour comedy. To wit, I have very little trouble imagining an alternate universe where Matt Tarses was allowed to become head writer and executive producer for a season 3 of _Sports Night_ — he did, after all, go on to write and co-exec for _Scrubs_, a half-hour comedy that theoretically owes its existence to _Sports Night_ with its no-laugh-track, single-camera format.

It would have been a good show... but it wouldn't have been an Aaron Sorkin show anymore — and that was likely what was on Showtime's minds when they pulled out of the deal upon being informed of his intentions. _Sports Night_ therefore officially ended its run after two seasons, so this is where I am obliged to provide my retrospective of the show.

At the end of last season, I expanded upon the thesis I introduced about a writer's duty to give the audience a reason to cheer for a television show's main characters by stating that we had cause to cheer for all our band of heroes by the end of the season, so naturally I should review whether the same remains true of season 2. Unfortunately, I will have to give this season a mixed review on that front. Dan's character gets perhaps the most proper development this season with his mental health arc (or arcs plural, depending on how you count) and through that development comes out with yet more reason for us to cheer for him, and as little as we see him Isaac's continuing struggles makes us hard pressed not to want the best for him. On the other hand, Natalie's character gets, somehow, thoroughly downgraded this season to the point where I am disinclined to wish the best for her, as much as there was an attempt to boost her character with the job interview mini-arc. Jeremy's character ends up as collateral damage from Natalie's implosion, with his treatment of Jenny afterward serving to sour our impression of him and not much coming after that to remedy. Dana's character goes through a roller coaster of audience likability this season, as her Dating Plan™ sent her character quite completely into the ground, but once that arc mercifully ended, her character's struggles with running the show well serve to bring her character back up to respectability, as random as the elements in those struggles may have seemed at times. Casey's character, unfortunately, gets rather flattened in the aftermath of the Dating Plan™, with his episode-by-episode purpose largely see-sawing between comic relief and simple foil to Dan's character — and I once again feel inclined to lament the complete absence of Charlie from this season, which would have served to three-dimensionalize Casey better.

On a different note, we of course cannot escape the trap of comparing _Sports Night_ to its sister Sorkin program _The West Wing_. The comparison perhaps feels especially apropos with this season, considering not only the shows were running simultaneously but also the former was able to ditch the tyranny of the laugh track from the previous season. Frankly, I'm inclined to set aside the comparison as one of apples and oranges, for a number of reasons. Nonetheless, there are people out there who will tell you that _Sports Night_ is better than _The West Wing_.

I can understand why they may say that. On the other hand, I can unequivocally say that they're wrong.

Now, once again, I should state the disclaimer that half-hour comedy generally isn't my cup of television tea, so I'm naturally disinclined to agree with the claim. However, I would argue that the same is true of Mr. Sorkin himself: his writing style, as it had evolved, simply doesn't fit the idiom of half-hour comedy as much as it does the hour-long drama format of all his other television shows. His contributions to half-hour comedy certainly did the idiom a service overall — and writers of half-hour comedy programs today would definitely do well to take some cues from him — but expecting the man himself to apply his own writing to that realm in the long run was simply never feasible. A half-hour simply isn't enough time to let his material breathe, as evidenced by how obvious the plot-thread confusion of season 2 of _Sports Night_ becomes in a thorough watch compared with season 1 of _The West Wing_. I suspect Mr. Sorkin understood that himself at the time, considering which show he chose to continue writing for — and as a result, which show I will be writing about to come.
